Taking product distribution out of a monolith

How we moved marketplace product distribution for thousands of brands out of a monolith, sent only what changed, and cut cloud cost per connection from about $45 to about $3.

Anonymised. I led this team as Senior Engineering Manager from the start. Numbers are rounded down.

Context

I worked at an integrator: a B2B commerce platform that connects brands to online marketplaces. Brands send their product data once, and the platform sends catalogue, stock and prices to every marketplace they sell on. One marketplace alone had about 20 sub-channels. Today that means more than 6,000 brand-to-channel connections.

All of this lived inside the monolith.

What hurt

  • Coupling. Every marketplace change was a monolith change and waited for a monolith release.
  • Speed. Full catalogues were sent every time. Account update runs took about 24 hours and could only run at night.
  • Cost. Cloud cost grew with every new connection.
  • Change. Each channel had its own logic, so adding a channel or sub-channel was expensive.

What we wanted

  • Release distribution independently of the monolith.
  • Send only what changed.
  • Make a new channel a matter of configuration, not code.
  • Clean data for every channel: mapping, translation, validation, enrichment.
  • Cloud-native, with cost visible to the team.

The design

  • Send only changes. A batch compares a hash of each product with the last one sent. Unchanged products are skipped. (ADR-002)
  • Small steps. Mapping, translation, validation and enrichment are separate steps, easy to change and test on their own.
  • One document per product. Each product is stored as one document, with metadata in a relational database. Any product can be re-sent or debugged on its own.
  • Fan-out. Each brand publishes to a topic, and each of its channels subscribes with its own queue. Adding a channel never touches the producer. Failed messages go to dead-letter queues instead of getting lost.
  • Templates per channel, managed in an admin console, so a new channel or sub-channel is mostly configuration.

Stack: AWS (serverless functions for the processing steps, containers for the 24/7 parts, SNS and SQS, PostgreSQL, S3, Redis for hashes, OpenSearch for logs), infrastructure as code with Terraform and CloudFormation.

Trade-offs we accepted

Serverless functions for processing stepsPay per burst, easy to composeHarder to trace; costly if load turns steady

Bringing the cost down

Cost work started with cloud cost retrospectives with the team. Everyone saw what each part of the system cost. The team then decided by value what to change and what to just watch. The biggest levers were processing only changed products, cutting network and secrets-lookup costs, tuning database IO, and picking the right managed service for each job. Cost per connection went from about $45 to about $3. Cost became a team metric, not a finance surprise.

Results

  • Account update runs from about 24 hours, night-only, to about 3 hours.
  • Cloud cost per connection from about $45 to about $3.
  • Under 3 seconds per product through the pipeline.
  • 6,000+ brand-to-channel connections through one channel-agnostic layer.
  • New channels onboarded through templates and configuration.

What I'd do differently

I'd use serverless only for the truly spiky steps and run the steady pipeline on containers. Chained functions worked for us because we only processed changes, but they are harder to trace and get costly at steady volume. We applied that lesson on the next integration we built: a modular monolith on Kubernetes instead of microservices and functions (ADR-003, ADR-004).

Questions I usually get

  • Why serverless for the pipeline? We expected product changes in bursts, not a steady stream. For that profile it was cheap and simple. See "What I'd do differently".
  • How did you handle failures? Queue retries, dead-letter queues, logging of the whole flow, and a troubleshooting view in the admin console that shows what happened to a single product.
  • How did you get the cost down? By making cost visible to the team first, then deciding by value. See "Bringing the cost down".
